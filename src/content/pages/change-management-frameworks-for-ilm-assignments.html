Change management theory is among the most frequently cited but most shallowly applied knowledge in ILM Level 5 assignments. Candidates working on Managing Change, Managing Improvement, or related units routinely name a framework (Kotter's eight steps, Lewin's three-stage model, McKinsey's 7S) and summarise what it says, without ever applying it to a specific, named change in their own organisation. That single gap between describing a framework and applying it to real evidence is the most common reason change management criteria are referred at Level 5. This guide sets out the three frameworks ILM assessors see most often, what each is actually for, how to apply each one to your own workplace change with the specificity assessors require, and how to move from application to critical evaluation at Level 7.

What Change Management Frameworks Does ILM Expect You to Know?

ILM does not prescribe a single change management framework. Assessment criteria typically require the candidate to "apply a recognised change management model" or "use appropriate tools to analyse organisational change," leaving the choice of framework to the candidate. In practice, three frameworks account for the large majority of ILM Level 5 submissions: Kotter's (1996) Eight-Step Change Model, a process framework for leading a change initiative from initiation to embedding; Lewin's (1947) three-stage model of Unfreeze–Change–Refreeze, typically paired with his Force Field Analysis diagnostic tool; and the McKinsey 7S framework (Waterman, Peters and Phillips, 1980), an organisational diagnostic tool rather than a change process.

The distinction between process models and diagnostic models matters more than most candidates realise. Kotter and Lewin describe a sequence of stages a change initiative moves through; McKinsey 7S describes seven interdependent elements of an organisation that must be assessed for alignment before, during, and after a change. Treating McKinsey 7S as a step-by-step change method ("Step 1: assess Strategy, Step 2: assess Structure") is a common misapplication that assessors flag, because the framework does not describe a change process at all; it describes what must be examined to understand whether a proposed change will succeed.

Kotter's Eight-Step Change Model: Applying It to Your ILM Assignment

Kotter's (1996) model sets out eight sequential steps for leading transformational change: establishing a sense of urgency, creating a guiding coalition, developing a strategic vision, enlisting a volunteer army, enabling action by removing barriers, generating short-term wins, sustaining acceleration, and instituting change by embedding it in organisational culture. Kotter's central argument is that most change efforts fail not because the change itself was wrong, but because organisations skip steps, typically moving straight to implementation without building sufficient urgency or a strong enough coalition of support first.

The Level 5 application standard requires the candidate to map a real change initiative from their own organisation against each of the eight steps, evidencing which steps were carried out (with specific detail: who was in the guiding coalition, what the short-term wins actually were) and which were skipped or under-resourced. A submission that states "my organisation created urgency for the change" without specific evidence (what communication was used, what data or events created the urgency, how staff responded) will not meet the criterion for critical application. A submission that identifies the specific step that was skipped (for example, no guiding coalition was formed before implementation began, resulting in middle-management resistance) and evaluates the consequences of that gap against Kotter's model demonstrates the analytical depth Level 5 requires.

Distinction-level application goes further: evaluating whether Kotter's linear, sequential structure fits the pace and nature of the change being analysed. Kotter's model was developed primarily from large-scale, planned organisational transformations; candidates working with smaller, faster, or more continuous change (rolling service improvements, iterative process changes) should acknowledge that the model's eight discrete stages may compress, overlap, or not apply cleanly, and explain what that mismatch reveals about the limitations of applying a large-transformation model to a smaller or more incremental change.

Lewin's Change Model and Force Field Analysis in ILM Context

Lewin's (1947) three-stage model describes change as a movement through Unfreeze (creating readiness for change by disrupting the current equilibrium), Change (the transition period itself, during which ambiguity and resistance are typically highest), and Refreeze (stabilising the new way of working so it becomes the new normal rather than reverting). Lewin's Force Field Analysis is the diagnostic tool most commonly paired with the Unfreeze stage: it identifies the driving forces pushing toward the change and the restraining forces resisting it, and proposes that change is achieved either by strengthening the driving forces, weakening the restraining forces, or both.

For an ILM Level 5 assignment, a strong Force Field Analysis names specific driving and restraining forces from the candidate's own organisational context, not generic categories. "Resistance to change" is not a restraining force with sufficient specificity; "the finance team's concern that the new reporting system would extend month-end close by two days, based on their experience with the previous system migration" is. Once forces are named specifically, the analytical task is to evaluate which forces carry the most weight and to justify the chosen intervention: did the change succeed because a driving force was strengthened (senior sponsorship was made visible) or because a restraining force was weakened (the specific concern was addressed directly)?

A common Level 5 error is applying the Unfreeze–Change–Refreeze structure without evidencing the Refreeze stage at all, describing how the change was introduced but never addressing whether or how the new way of working was embedded, monitored, or reinforced. Since sustainability of change is frequently a specific assessment criterion, omitting Refreeze evidence leaves that criterion unmet regardless of how strong the Unfreeze and Change evidence is.

McKinsey 7S Framework for ILM Organisational Diagnosis

The McKinsey 7S framework identifies seven interdependent elements that must be aligned for an organisation to function effectively: Strategy, Structure, Systems, Shared Values, Skills, Staff, and Style. The framework's core proposition is that changing one element without considering its effect on the other six is a common cause of change failure. For example, introducing a new strategy (Strategy) without adjusting the organisational structure, systems, or skills needed to deliver it will produce misalignment regardless of how sound the strategy itself is.

ILM Level 5 assignments most commonly use McKinsey 7S in the Managing Improvement unit, as a diagnostic tool applied before proposing a change, rather than as the change process itself. A strong application audits the candidate's organisation (or a specific department or team) against each of the seven elements, identifies which elements are currently misaligned with a proposed or recent change, and evaluates the consequences of that misalignment. For example: a new performance management Strategy introduced without corresponding development of managers' appraisal Skills, or without adjusting the Systems (the appraisal software and process itself) that support it, produces a predictable implementation gap that 7S analysis surfaces directly.

Because McKinsey 7S is diagnostic rather than sequential, it pairs well with Kotter or Lewin in the same assignment: 7S identifies which elements of the organisation are misaligned and need to change, while Kotter or Lewin provides the process for leading that change through to completion. Using 7S alone, without a process framework, leaves the "how" of implementing the identified changes unaddressed. This is itself a common gap in Level 5 submissions that rely on 7S exclusively.

How to Choose the Right Change Framework for Your ILM Unit

Framework selection should be driven by the unit's specific assessment criteria and the nature of the change being analysed, not by which framework the candidate happens to remember. Kotter suits large-scale, leadership-driven transformation with a clear beginning and end: restructuring, system-wide implementations, mergers. Lewin and Force Field Analysis suit discrete, bounded changes with an identifiable before-state and after-state, and are particularly well suited to units that ask the candidate to analyse resistance to change specifically. McKinsey 7S suits units that ask for organisational analysis or diagnosis rather than a change implementation narrative. It is the strongest choice for "Managing Improvement" style criteria that ask the candidate to identify what needs to change, rather than "Managing Change" criteria that ask the candidate to evaluate how a change was led.

There is no requirement to use only one framework. A well-constructed Level 5 or Level 7 assignment often uses one framework as the primary analytical lens and a second as a point of comparison or a diagnostic input: using 7S to identify organisational misalignment, then Kotter to evaluate how (or whether) that misalignment was addressed through a structured change process.

Applying Change Theory to Your Own Organisation: What Assessors Look For

Across all three frameworks, the assessment standard is the same: named, specific, evidenced application to the candidate's own organisational context, not description of the framework in the abstract. Assessors are explicitly trained to distinguish between a paragraph that explains what Kotter's eight steps are and a paragraph that uses Kotter's eight steps to structure an analysis of a specific change the candidate was involved in. The first is descriptive; the second is analytical, and only the second meets ILM's Level 5 assessment criteria for critical analysis and application.

Practically, this means every framework element referenced in the assignment should be followed by a specific piece of evidence: a stakeholder named or role-described, a document referenced, a timeframe given, an outcome stated. Where the candidate does not have a real change initiative to analyse, for example, if they have not yet led a significant change, a realistic case study drawn from their own department, evaluated with the same specificity, is an acceptable substitute, provided the assignment brief permits it. What is not acceptable is a generic, hypothetical "a company might" narrative that could apply to any organisation. This is the clearest signal to an assessor that the theory has not actually been applied.

Critiquing Change Frameworks at ILM Level 7

Level 7 assessment requires more than accurate application: it requires critical synthesis, including an honest evaluation of each framework's limitations and, where appropriate, comparison between frameworks. Kotter's model has been critiqued for its linear, sequential structure, which assumes change moves through discrete phases in order; in practice, particularly in agile or continuously changing environments, phases frequently overlap or recur, and organisations report cycling back to "building urgency" activities well after implementation has begun. Lewin's model has been critiqued on similar grounds: the assumption of a stable "refrozen" end-state fits industries with infrequent, large-scale change poorly when applied to organisations undergoing continuous, iterative change, where a genuinely stable new equilibrium may never be reached before the next change begins.

McKinsey 7S is frequently critiqued for being comprehensive but not prescriptive: it tells you what to examine, not what to do once misalignment is found, which is why Level 7 candidates who rely on 7S alone are typically marked down for failing to connect the diagnosis to a concrete strategic response. A strong Level 7 submission acknowledges these limitations explicitly, rather than presenting any single framework as a complete or unproblematic account of organisational change, and uses the identified limitation to justify triangulating more than one framework against the same organisational evidence.

Change Management vs Change Leadership: What ILM Level 5 vs Level 7 Expects

A distinction candidates frequently miss is that ILM Level 5 units generally assess change management (the structured process of planning, implementing, and embedding a specific change) while ILM Level 7 units increasingly assess change leadership, which is concerned with the leader's role in enabling and sustaining an organisational capacity for continuous change, not just delivering one discrete initiative. Applying Kotter or Lewin correctly to a single named change is sufficient evidence at Level 5. At Level 7, the same theoretical grounding needs to be extended to address how the candidate, as a senior leader, builds organisational readiness and resilience for change generally, connecting to strategic leadership frameworks and to the candidate's own leadership development, not only to the mechanics of one project.

Common Change Management Assignment Errors at ILM Level 5

Beyond description without application, three further errors recur across referred Level 5 change management submissions: using a framework element as a heading without discussing it (listing "Systems" as a 7S heading followed by unrelated content); applying a framework to a change that has not yet happened or is entirely hypothetical, which leaves the assessor unable to verify that genuine analysis rather than speculation has occurred; and omitting the evaluation of outcomes altogether: describing what was done without stating whether the change actually achieved its intended result, and why. Each of these gaps corresponds to a specific, addressable fix rather than requiring a wholesale rewrite. Change Management Frameworks for ILM Assignments: Frequently Asked Questions

Which change management model should I use for my ILM Level 5 assignment?

Choose based on the unit's specific assessment criteria and the nature of the change. Kotter's Eight-Step Model suits a large, leadership-driven transformation with a clear beginning and end. Lewin's model and Force Field Analysis suit a discrete change where resistance and driving/restraining forces are central to the criterion. McKinsey 7S suits organisational diagnosis units, identifying what needs to change, rather than units that ask you to evaluate how a change was led. If in doubt, check whether the unit language says "analyse" or "diagnose" (favouring 7S) versus "lead" or "implement" (favouring Kotter or Lewin).

Can I use more than one change framework in the same ILM assignment?

Yes, and at Level 7 it is often expected. A common and effective structure uses McKinsey 7S to diagnose which elements of the organisation are misaligned, then Kotter or Lewin to evaluate the process used (or that should be used) to address that misalignment. Using two frameworks together, each applied to the same evidence with a clear rationale for why each was chosen, demonstrates stronger analytical range than relying on a single framework throughout.

Do I need to apply the framework to a real workplace change, or can I use a hypothetical example?

ILM's work-based assessment model expects application to real or realistic evidence from your own organisation. A genuinely hypothetical, generic example ("a company might implement a new system") will not meet the criterion for critical application, because it cannot be evidenced with the specificity assessors require. If you have not led a significant change yourself, a case study drawn from your own department or team, evaluated with the same level of specific detail as a real initiative, is generally acceptable, provided your unit brief permits it. Confirm with your tutor if you are uncertain.
