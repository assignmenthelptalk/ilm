Coaching supervision is one of the more specific and confusing requirements in ILM Level 5 and Level 7 Coaching and Mentoring qualifications, largely because most candidates have never encountered the concept before and understandably confuse it with being supervised by their manager at work. This guide explains what coaching supervision actually is, why ILM requires it, what happens in a session, and how to document it correctly as portfolio evidence.

What Is Coaching Supervision? (And How It Differs From Workplace Supervision)

Coaching supervision is a reflective, confidential professional conversation between a coach and a more experienced coach-supervisor, focused specifically on the coach's own coaching practice — not on the coach's day job, and not on the coachees they work with directly as the primary subject. It exists to support the coach's own development as a practitioner, provide space to process difficult or "stuck" coaching relationships, and maintain ethical and professional accountability, in line with the standards set by professional coaching bodies such as the ICF (International Coaching Federation) and EMCC (European Mentoring and Coaching Council), which ILM coaching units frequently reference.

This is entirely distinct from workplace line-management supervision — the regular check-ins or performance conversations you might have with your own manager about your day job. Coaching supervision is specifically about your coaching practice, delivered by someone qualified to supervise coaches, and its confidentiality expectations are closer to clinical or therapeutic supervision than to a standard workplace one-to-one.

Why ILM Requires Documented Coaching Supervision at Level 5 and Level 7

ILM Coaching and Mentoring qualifications assess not just whether you can coach effectively, but whether you can reflect critically on your own coaching practice in an ongoing, professionally supported way — supervision is the recognised professional mechanism for that ongoing critical reflection, and evidencing your engagement with it demonstrates a level of professional maturity beyond simply completing coaching sessions. Requiring documented supervision also connects your ILM qualification to the wider professional coaching standards (ICF, EMCC) that will matter to your credibility as a coach beyond the qualification itself.

What Happens in a Coaching Supervision Session

A supervision session is typically structured around a specific coaching relationship or session the coach found challenging or is still processing — an ethical dilemma encountered, a pattern the coach notices recurring across their coaching practice, or the coach's own emotional response to a difficult coaching conversation (sometimes called the parallel process, where the coach's own feelings mirror something happening in the coaching relationship itself). The supervisor's role is not to give direct answers but to help the coach reflect more deeply on their own practice, using many of the same reflective questioning techniques the coach themselves uses with their coachees.

How to Write a Coaching Supervision Log for Your ILM Portfolio

A supervision log for your ILM portfolio should record: the date and duration of the session, your supervisor's name and relevant qualification or experience, the general topic or theme discussed (in appropriately anonymised form — see below), and, most importantly, your own reflection on what the session revealed about your coaching practice and what you will do differently as a result. This final reflective element is what turns a bare attendance record into genuine portfolio evidence — a log that only states "I discussed a difficult coaching relationship with my supervisor" without any reflection on what you learned from that discussion does not meet the evidential depth ILM assessment criteria require.

How Often Do You Need Supervision, and in What Format?

The required frequency and format (one-to-one supervision versus group supervision with other coaches) vary between ILM centres and the specific qualification you are studying, so confirm the exact requirement directly with your training provider rather than assume a universal standard applies. Both formats are professionally recognised — group supervision has the additional benefit of hearing how other coaches process similar dilemmas, while one-to-one supervision allows more sustained focus on your own specific practice.

Anonymising Coachee Information in Your Supervision Documentation

Because supervision discussion often involves real, sensitive material about a specific coachee, your documented log must anonymise the coachee — removing names and any identifying detail — while retaining enough real structure (the general nature of the coaching challenge, the dynamic involved, the outcome) for your ILM assessor to evaluate genuine reflective depth. This is the same anonymisation skill required for work-based evidence elsewhere in ILM coaching units, and the same standard applies: specific enough to demonstrate real engagement, anonymised enough to protect the coachee.

Connecting Supervision Logs to Your Reflective Practice

A strong coaching supervision log uses the same reflective structure as any other ILM reflective account — a specific (anonymised) situation, your own evaluation of it, and a forward-looking action for your practice — which means the Gibbs, Kolb, or Schon reflective models you may already be using elsewhere in your ILM coaching assignments apply equally well to structuring your supervision documentation.

Supervision as Part of Your Wider Coaching Portfolio

Your supervision log sits alongside your coaching session logs, client consent records, and reflective accounts as one part of a complete ILM coaching and mentoring portfolio — each evidencing a different dimension of your coaching competence, from the coaching sessions themselves through to the ongoing professional reflection that supervision specifically provides. ILM Coaching Supervision: Frequently Asked Questions

Is coaching supervision the same as being supervised by my manager at work?

No. Coaching supervision is a distinct, confidential professional conversation with a qualified coach-supervisor about your coaching practice specifically, following professional standards similar to clinical supervision. It is entirely separate from your regular line-management supervision or appraisal conversations at work, which are about your day job rather than your coaching practice.

How many supervision sessions do I need to document for my ILM portfolio?

The required frequency varies by ILM centre and qualification, so confirm the specific requirement directly with your training provider. What matters most for each documented session is not the count alone but the depth of reflection recorded — a smaller number of well-reflected sessions is more valuable evidence than a larger number of thin, attendance-only records.

What if I don't have access to a qualified coaching supervisor?

Speak to your ILM training provider directly — most centres either provide access to a supervisor as part of the programme or can direct you to appropriate options, since supervision access is a standard requirement they are used to supporting candidates through. Do not substitute an unqualified colleague or your own line manager for a genuine coaching supervisor, as this will not meet the assessment requirement.
