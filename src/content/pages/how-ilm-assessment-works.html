Understanding exactly how ILM assessment works before you start — rather than discovering the process midway through your first unit — makes a real difference to how you approach your first assignment. ILM does not assess through exams; it assesses through work-based assignments and portfolios, marked against defined criteria by a named assessor, with a formal quality assurance process behind every decision. This guide explains the assignment and portfolio model, who is actually involved in marking your work, what happens between submission and result, and why work-based evidence is judged differently from a conventional academic essay.

The ILM Assessment Model: Assignments and Portfolios, Not Exams

Nearly all ILM qualifications are assessed through written assignments and work-based evidence portfolios rather than timed, closed-book examinations. This is a deliberate design choice: ILM qualifications are intended to certify demonstrated management and leadership competence in a real workplace, not the ability to recall and apply theory under exam conditions. Each unit within an ILM qualification has its own assignment brief and its own set of assessment criteria — a Level 5 Diploma, for example, is not one large assessment but a series of separate unit assignments, each independently marked and each requiring a pass before the unit is awarded.

The practical implication is that ILM assessment rewards sustained, evidenced application over time far more than it rewards a single strong performance under pressure. A candidate builds their portfolio unit by unit, typically over weeks or months per unit depending on the programme structure, drawing on real events from their own management practice as they occur rather than constructing a hypothetical scenario from scratch.

Who Assesses Your ILM Work: Tutor, Assessor, and Quality Assurer Roles

Three distinct roles exist in the ILM assessment chain, and candidates frequently conflate the first two. The tutor teaches the unit content and may offer informal, formative guidance on a draft — helping the candidate understand what a criterion means and what kind of evidence would be appropriate. The assessor formally marks the submitted assignment or portfolio against the unit's specific learning outcomes and assessment criteria, and makes the pass or refer decision. In many ILM centres the tutor and assessor are the same person for a given unit, but the two functions are distinct: teaching and marking are governed by different expectations, and a tutor acting as assessor must apply the criteria objectively regardless of what informal guidance they gave earlier.

Above the assessor sits the centre's internal quality assurer (IQA), who samples assessment decisions within the training centre to check they are applied consistently across candidates and across different assessors. Above the centre, City and Guilds appoints external verifiers (EVs) who sample across multiple centres nationally to confirm that assessment standards are consistent with the national specification for the qualification — not just consistent within one centre. This layered structure exists specifically so that a candidate's outcome does not depend on which centre or which individual assessor happened to mark their work.

The Assignment Submission and Marking Cycle: Step by Step

The typical cycle runs as follows: the candidate receives the unit's assignment brief and assessment criteria at the start of the unit; the candidate gathers work-based evidence and drafts their submission over the course of the unit, in some centres with access to formative tutor feedback on a draft; the candidate submits the final assignment along with any portfolio evidence and a signed authentication declaration confirming the work is their own; the assessor marks the submission against each assessment criterion individually, recording for each criterion whether it has been met; and the assessor issues an outcome — most ILM units are Pass/Refer only, without a Merit or Distinction tier at unit level, though some qualifications or specific units do recognise higher achievement.

Feedback timelines vary by centre and are not centrally fixed by City and Guilds, so candidates should confirm the expected turnaround directly with their centre or tutor at the start of the programme rather than assume a standard window. Where the outcome is Pass, the unit is complete. Where the outcome is Refer, the candidate receives feedback identifying which specific criteria were not met, with a resubmission opportunity within a timeframe set by the centre's own assessment policy.

What Happens After You Submit: Pass, Refer, and Feedback Timelines

A referral is not a fail — it is a decision that the evidence submitted does not yet fully demonstrate that a specific criterion, or set of criteria, has been met, with an opportunity to resubmit improved or additional evidence. City and Guilds quality assurance standards constrain what an assessor can say in referral feedback: they can identify which criteria are unmet and indicate the general nature of the gap (missing evidence, insufficient analytical depth, theory not applied), but they cannot write the candidate's response for them or specify exactly what content would guarantee a pass, since doing so would undermine the assessment's ability to certify the candidate's own competence.

This constraint is the direct reason candidates often need external guidance in interpreting referral feedback and planning a resubmission — the feedback tells you what is missing without telling you precisely how to fill the gap, and translating "insufficient critical analysis" into a specific, targeted revision is a skill in itself. How Work-Based Learning Is Assessed Differently From Academic Coursework

A conventional academic essay is judged primarily on the quality of argument, evidence of reading, and structure of the writing. ILM evidence is judged on those things too, but with an additional and equally important dimension: authenticity to the candidate's real workplace practice. A technically well-written, theoretically sound paragraph describing a generic or hypothetical management scenario can fail an ILM criterion that a less polished but specifically evidenced account of a real workplace situation will pass — because the criterion is asking the candidate to demonstrate what they actually did or would do in their own context, supported by real evidence, not to demonstrate that they can write convincingly about management in the abstract.

This is why ILM portfolios often include supporting evidence beyond the written assignment itself — witness statements from managers or colleagues, minutes or documents from real meetings, records of workplace activities — mapped explicitly against the criteria they support. The written analysis and the supporting evidence work together: the evidence establishes that the situation being analysed genuinely occurred, and the written analysis demonstrates the candidate's critical engagement with it.

Quality Assurance in ILM: Internal and External Sampling

Internal quality assurance (IQA) sampling exists to catch inconsistency between assessors within the same centre before it reaches the candidate as an unfair outcome — an IQA reviewing a sample of assessment decisions might identify that one assessor is marking more strictly or more leniently than others against the same criteria, and correct that inconsistency going forward. External verification (EV) exists to catch the same kind of inconsistency at a national level, comparing assessment standards across different centres delivering the same ILM qualification, so that a Level 5 pass from one centre represents a genuinely comparable standard of competence to a Level 5 pass from another.

For candidates, the practical significance of this structure is reassurance rather than day-to-day relevance: assessment decisions are not solely dependent on one individual's judgement, and a formal route exists — through the centre and then through City and Guilds — for challenging a decision believed to be inconsistent, procedurally flawed, or biased. This is distinct from a routine resubmission following a referral, which does not require any claim that the original decision was wrong.

What This Means for How You Should Prepare Your ILM Assignment

Understanding this process before you start changes how you should approach your first ILM assignment: begin gathering real workplace evidence as events happen, rather than trying to reconstruct a strong example retrospectively when the deadline approaches; read the specific assessment criteria for your unit before drafting, since the criteria — not the general topic area — define exactly what your assessor is looking for; and treat any formative feedback from your tutor on a draft as a genuine opportunity to close gaps before final submission, since that opportunity does not exist in the same way once the assignment has been formally submitted and marked.

How This Process Differs Across ILM Levels

The assessment model described here is consistent across ILM levels — assignments and portfolios marked against defined criteria, with IQA and EV sampling behind every decision. What changes by level is the depth and analytical standard the criteria require: Level 3 criteria typically ask the candidate to understand and apply theory to their own team context with examples; Level 5 criteria ask for critical analysis, evaluation, and Harvard-referenced theoretical grounding; Level 7 criteria ask for critical synthesis across multiple theoretical frameworks at postgraduate depth. The marking process and quality assurance structure is the same throughout — only the standard being assessed against changes. How ILM Assessment Works: Frequently Asked Questions

Does ILM use exams, or is everything assignment-based?

Nearly all ILM qualifications are assessed entirely through written assignments and work-based portfolio evidence rather than timed exams. Each unit within a qualification has its own assignment brief and criteria, marked independently by your assessor. This reflects ILM's design purpose — certifying demonstrated management competence in a real workplace, not exam performance.

How long does it take to get feedback on an ILM assignment?

Feedback timelines are set by each centre's own assessment policy rather than fixed centrally by City and Guilds, so turnaround varies between centres and programmes. Confirm the expected timeline directly with your tutor or centre coordinator at the start of your programme, and again immediately if you have not heard back within the timeframe you were given.

What is the difference between my ILM tutor and my ILM assessor?

The tutor teaches unit content and may give informal, formative guidance on a draft. The assessor formally marks your final submission against the assessment criteria and issues the pass or refer decision. In many centres the same person performs both roles for a given unit, but the two functions are governed differently — formative guidance from a tutor is not the same as a final assessment decision from an assessor, even when it is the same individual.
